Course description

The aim of the lecture is to prepare future lawyers to critically assess the impact of artificial intelligence (AI) on society, culture and the legal order, and to use AI tools in legal work in an informed way.

The lecture combines three perspectives. The first is technological and historical: what AI is, how it developed and how contemporary models, including large language models, work. The second concerns social and cultural effects: work and education, ethics and algorithmic bias, cultural differences in approaches to technology, and environmental costs. The third, and the most extensive, is legal: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 13 June 2024 laying down harmonised rules on artificial intelligence (Artificial Intelligence Act, ‘AI Act’) and the Polish ustawa z dnia 3 lipca 2026 r. o systemach sztucznej inteligencji (Act of 3 July 2026 on Artificial Intelligence Systems), Dz.U. 2026, item 1003 (‘Polish AI Systems Act’), personal data protection, copyright, civil and criminal liability, and the use of AI in the administration of justice and the legal professions.

Case studies and case law are discussed within each thematic block. The lecture draws on the findings of research conducted by a WPiA UMCS team on the attitudes towards AI of lawyers (‘Algorytm w Todze’ [The Algorithm in the Robe]) and of school pupils and students (‘Algorytm w Ławce Szkolnej’ [The Algorithm at the School Desk]). Materials, slides, quizzes and interactive applications are available on the course website.

Course content

Ten topics of 1.5 h each over nine meetings. Topic 4 is split between two longer meetings: the part on work (6 November 2026, together with topic 3) and the part on education (20 November 2026, together with topic 5). Meeting dates are on the course website (map and list of districts).

Topic 1. Introduction. What is artificial intelligence?

  • Organisational matters: assessment rules, course website, policy on the use of AI in class.
  • Definitions of AI: Turing, McCarthy, OECD, the definition of an AI system in Article 3(1) AI Act.
  • Basic concepts: model, training data, parameters, machine learning, deep learning, natural language processing, generative AI.
  • ANI, AGI, ASI. The Turing test and its contemporary critiques.
  • Overview of AI applications, including in legal practice. AI literacy (Article 4 AI Act).

Topic 2. History of artificial intelligence: from automata to foundation models

  • Prehistory: myths, automata, Llull and Leibniz, the Analytical Engine.
  • Foundations: the McCulloch–Pitts neuron model (1943), Turing (1950), the Dartmouth conference (1956), Rosenblatt’s perceptron (1958).
  • The golden years (1956–1974), the first AI winter (1974–1980), the renaissance of expert systems (1980–1987), including the first legal expert systems, the second AI winter (1987–1993).
  • Breakthroughs: Deep Blue (1997), ImageNet (2012), AlphaGo and ‘move 37’ (2016), the transformer architecture (2017), ChatGPT (2022), AI agents (2024–2026).

Topic 3. How does AI work? Technological foundations for lawyers

  • From rules to data: expert systems versus machine learning.
  • Supervised, unsupervised and reinforcement learning.
  • Neural networks and deep learning. The brain and the neural network: the limits of the analogy.
  • Large language models: tokens, next-token prediction, the attention mechanism.
  • Hallucinations and their causes. The black-box problem and explainable AI (XAI).
  • General-purpose AI models (GPAI), multimodal models, AI agents.

Topic 4. Social challenges of AI (I): work and education

  • Automation versus augmentation of work. The ‘jagged frontier’ of technology: research by BCG and Harvard Business School.
  • Sectors and occupations most affected by the change, including the legal professions.
  • Invisible data work, precarisation, proposals for a basic income.
  • Education: academic integrity, the ‘illusion of cognitive efficiency’, findings of the ‘Algorytm w Ławce Szkolnej’ study.
  • Reskilling and employees’ digital skills.

Topic 5. Social challenges of AI (II): ethics, bias, transparency

  • Ethical frameworks for AI: the guidelines of the High-Level Expert Group (2019), the UNESCO Recommendation (2021), the OECD principles.
  • Algorithmic bias: recruitment, recidivism risk assessment (State v. Loomis), the SyRI system (the Netherlands).
  • Transparency and explainability of decisions: Article 22 GDPR, the judgments of the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) in SCHUFA (C-634/21) and Dun & Bradstreet Austria (C-203/22).
  • Content moderation and private censorship: the SIN v Meta case, DSA mechanisms.
  • Disinformation and deepfakes. Optimising for engagement versus truth (‘Moloch’s Bargain’).

Topic 6. Cultural challenges of AI: regulation, language, creativity

  • Regulation as a cultural choice: risk-based (EU), impact- and development-based (South Korea, Japan), market-driven (USA) and state-led (China) approaches.
  • Cognitive heuristics and game theory in the making of AI regulation.
  • Language and culture in training data: the dominance of English, Polish language models (PLLuM, Bielik), local values in global systems.
  • AI and creativity: machine creativity, generative art, deepfakes in culture.
  • Images of AI in culture: Lem, Asimov, ‘Blade Runner’, ‘Black Mirror’. Anthropomorphisation.

Topic 7. Legal challenges of AI (I): regulatory framework and copyright

  • AI Act: definitions, the risk-based approach, prohibited AI practices, high-risk AI systems, transparency obligations (Article 50), general-purpose AI models.
  • The timeline for the application of the AI Act following the amendments introduced by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744.
  • The Act of 3 July 2026 on Artificial Intelligence Systems: the Commission for the Development and Safety of Artificial Intelligence (KRiBSI), inspections, sanctions, regulatory sandboxes.
  • The Council of Europe Framework Convention on Artificial Intelligence and Human Rights, Democracy and the Rule of Law (CETS No. 225). The DSA and Regulation (EU) 2022/1925 (Digital Markets Act, DMA) as the wider regulatory environment.
  • Copyright: whether AI output is a work, text and data mining (TDM) in model training, litigation (Thaler v. Perlmutter, Getty Images v. Stability AI, GEMA v. OpenAI, New York Times v. OpenAI and Microsoft, Bartz v. Anthropic).

Topic 8. Legal challenges of AI (II): personal data, liability, administration of justice

  • Personal data protection and AI: legal bases for processing, data minimisation, data subject rights, data protection impact assessment, automated individual decision-making.
  • Privacy violations by AI systems: facial recognition, profiling, decisions of supervisory authorities.
  • Civil liability for damage caused by AI: liability regimes under the Civil Code (k.c.), the new Directive (EU) 2024/2853 on liability for defective products (new Product Liability Directive), the withdrawal of the proposed AI Liability Directive.
  • Criminal liability. The debate on legal personhood for AI.
  • AI in the administration of justice and the legal professions: high-risk AI systems, hallucinated case law in court filings (Mata v. Avianca), findings of the ‘Algorytm w Todze’ study, standards of professional ethics.

Topic 9. The future of AI: trends, scenarios, deployments

  • Moore’s law, scaling laws and the pace of change. The technological singularity.
  • Development scenarios (including ‘AI 2027’) and AI researchers’ forecasts.
  • Techno-optimism and techno-scepticism. The alignment problem.
  • Case studies of AI deployments: successes, failures, lessons learned and good practice.

Topic 10. AI and sustainable development. Course summary

  • The energy and water footprint of AI. Data centres and the energy system.
  • Raw materials and supply chains: cobalt, tin, lithium. The historical precedent of gutta-percha.
  • The ‘logic of extraction’ (K. Crawford) and global inequalities.
  • AI applications for the climate and the environment. Rules on environmental impact reporting.
  • Course summary and preparation for assessment.

Teaching methods

  • Expository methods: informative lecture, explanation, multimedia presentation.
  • Problem-based methods: problem-based lecture, case study, analysis of judgments, SWOT analysis, decision trees, discussion.
  • Activating methods: tasks with generative models, interactive applications and quizzes on the course website, live polls.

Assessment methods and criteria

Form of assessment: graded credit. Students choose one of three paths.

Path A: written test.

  • 20 single-choice questions (1 point each) and 2 open questions (5 points each), 30 points in total.
  • The open questions test the ability to apply legal provisions to a short set of facts and to critically assess a problem.

Path B: course project.

  • A concept for an AI-based application that helps lawyers with a specific task, together with an implementation plan (a 4–5-page document plus annexes: a flow diagram and draft screens; a prototype is optional). The idea for the application must be agreed with the lecturer by 6 November 2026.
  • The project covers: the problem and the user, the application concept, a legal analysis (AI Act, GDPR, professional secrecy, liability, professional ethics), risks and safeguards, and the implementation plan.
  • Criteria: application concept and its usefulness 30%, legal and ethical analysis 30%, implementation plan, risks and human oversight 20%, sources, structure and editing 20%.
  • The use of AI tools in the project is permitted provided that the student attaches a statement specifying which tools were used and to what extent. Students are responsible for the accuracy of all sources cited.

Path C: student’s own proposal.

  • A form of work of the student’s own choosing related to the subject matter of the course (e.g. a prototype AI tool for lawyers, an analysis of a judgment or a case, a popular-science article, an educational podcast or film), submitted with a short description by 6 November 2026 and approved by the lecturer.
  • Scope comparable to the Path B project, with a short written commentary (1–2 pages) setting out the legal basis and sources; the assessment criteria are set by the lecturer upon approval.
  • The use of AI tools is permitted provided that a statement is attached.
